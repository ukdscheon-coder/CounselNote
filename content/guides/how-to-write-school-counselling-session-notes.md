Session notes are one of the few parts of school counselling that other people may one day read: a Designated Safeguarding Lead (DSL), a data protection officer handling a subject access request, a court, or the young person themselves as an adult. Writing them well protects the pupil, protects you, and makes your own work easier to review.

This guide sets out what good practice looks like in a UK school setting. It is general information, not legal advice — your school's record-keeping, safeguarding and data protection policies always take priority.

## Why session notes matter in a school

In a school, counselling sits inside a wider safeguarding system. The Department for Education's statutory guidance *Keeping Children Safe in Education* (KCSIE) expects all staff to record concerns about a child's welfare and pass them to the DSL. Your counselling notes are separate from the school's child-protection record, but they are still personal data about a child under UK GDPR, and they may be requested, disclosed or scrutinised.

Good notes therefore do three jobs:

1. **Continuity of care** — they remind you what was agreed, what was said and what you planned next.
2. **Accountability** — they show that your decisions (including decisions *not* to share information) were reasoned and made at the time.
3. **Safeguarding** — they provide an accurate, dated account if a concern escalates.

## What to include in every session record

A consistent structure makes notes quicker to write and easier to read later. A minimal record for each session usually covers:

| Section | What to write |
|---|---|
| Date, time and length | When the session took place and how long it lasted, including whether it was cut short. |
| Session number and format | e.g. "Session 4 of 6, in person, counselling room". |
| Attendance | Attended, did not attend, cancelled — and by whom. Non-attendance can itself be meaningful. |
| Presenting themes | Brief summary of what the young person brought, in neutral language. |
| Key content | Significant statements, especially anything relevant to risk, recorded in the pupil's own words where it matters. |
| Your observations | What you observed (appearance, affect, behaviour) described factually. |
| Risk check | Whether risk was assessed, the outcome, and any action taken. |
| Outcome measure scores | If you use measures such as SDQ or YP-CORE, the score and date. |
| Actions and plan | Agreed next steps, who owns them and by when, and the plan for the next session. |

## Separate fact, what was said, and your professional view

The most common weakness in counselling notes is blending observation, report and interpretation into a single sentence. Compare:

- *Weak:* "J was clearly lying about home and seemed depressed."
- *Better:* "J said, 'Everything's fine at home, I just don't sleep.' J made little eye contact and spoke quietly throughout. My view: low mood is possible; I will repeat the wellbeing measure next session."

The better version records **what was said** (in quotation marks), **what you observed**, and **your professional opinion**, clearly labelled. Anyone reading it later can see where each part came from.

Some practical rules of thumb:

- Use the young person's exact words for any disclosure or statement about harm.
- Avoid labels and diagnoses you are not qualified to make.
- Avoid judgemental adjectives ("manipulative", "attention-seeking").
- Write as if the pupil, their parent or a judge may read it — because they might.

## Write contemporaneously

Notes should be written as soon as practicable after the session, ideally the same day. Record the date and time you wrote the note if it differs from the session time. If you add information later, add a new dated entry rather than altering the original — changing an earlier record without an audit trail can undermine its credibility.

## Process notes versus the formal record

Many counsellors keep brief personal "process notes" — reflections, hypotheses, feelings evoked in the session — to use in supervision. Be aware that under UK GDPR, if these notes identify a pupil they are still personal data and may fall within a subject access request, wherever they are kept. The safest approach is to keep one formal record, written to the standard above, and to take identifiable detail out of anything you bring to supervision.

## When a session raises a safeguarding concern

If a pupil says or shows something that suggests they may be at risk of harm:

1. Record exactly what was said and seen, with the date and time, as soon as possible.
2. Follow your school's safeguarding procedure and speak to the DSL without delay — do not wait until your notes are finished if the concern is urgent.
3. Record in your counselling notes that you made the referral, to whom, when, and what was shared.
4. The DSL will record the concern in the school's child-protection system (for example CPOMS or MyConcern, or a paper file). Your counselling record does not replace that system.

See also our guides on [confidentiality and safeguarding](confidentiality-and-safeguarding-in-school-counselling.html) and [what to share with the DSL](sharing-information-with-the-designated-safeguarding-lead.html).

## Keep notes proportionate

More is not always better. Long, speculative notes are harder to review and may disclose more than necessary if they are ever shared. Record what is needed for continuity, accountability and safeguarding — and no more. The UK GDPR principle of **data minimisation** applies to counselling records as much as to any other personal data.

## A reusable template

You can copy this structure into whichever system you use:

```
Date / time / length:
Session no. / format:
Attendance:
Themes brought:
Key statements (pupil's own words where relevant):
Observations:
Risk check (done? outcome? action?):
Measures (name, score):
My professional view (labelled as opinion):
Actions (who / what / by when):
Plan for next session:
Note written: date / time / by
```
